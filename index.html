    Quito, the capital of Ecuador, lies in a long, narrow basin of the northern Andes, about 2 800 m above the sea, on top of the Quito Fault System: reverse faults, mostly blind, running under the city from north to south.

    For the same earthquake, two sites do not record the same shaking: the local soil profile and the surface topography change it. That variability, the site effect, is what this work simulates, one medium at a time, and what the neural surrogate learns to reproduce.

    Instead of a synthetic domain, the model uses a cross-section of Quito's real topography, from the Copernicus GLO-30 elevation model of the Metropolitan District at 31 m. The city fills a long basin between the Pichincha volcano and the Quito Fault System, reverse faults dipping west beneath it.     The fault traces and planes, the zones and the urban outline are context only: in the code they can never reach the solver (solver_allowed = false). The rupture the solver uses is parametric and fixed.

    The simulated earthquake lives in one vertical plane: the section A–B beneath Segment 3. The solver is 2D (plane strain), so the wave it computes propagates in that plane and nowhere else. Here the terrain turns transparent, the relief settles to its true height (1:1), the section opens, and the replayed wavefield runs through the medium while the rupture lights up and the 39 surface receivers shake with their own recorded acceleration.

    From the terrain to the grid

    Three steps, and only one vector of integers reaches the solver: the row where the solid starts in each column.

    1. The section A–B is sampled from the elevation model and cut into pieces of 4 km.
    2. Segment 3, which crosses the deepest ravine, is interpolated onto the grid's 279 columns without stretching.
    3. The highest point is z = 0 and every column is rounded to the nearest row: a staircase of whole cells. The vertical error is at most half a cell.

    The network was trained on 1,000 randomly layered media beneath Quito's topography along Segment 3: 6 to 12 layers each, the deepest reaching the model's floor, each layer with its own Vp, Vs and density (ρ), and every interface draped under the terrain. The six media shown here come from the held-out test split: the network never saw them during training.

    The source is not a point: it is a kinematic rupture, the same in all 1,000 media. It is discretized into 45 subsources along a blind fault, triggered one after another as the rupture front travels down-dip from the hypocentre, each emitting the same Ricker pulse. Each subsource pushes with its own share of the total force, largest in the middle and zero at the tips.

    On the block, every subsource is a disc sized by its share; it lights up when the front reaches it and sends out a ring, its copy of the outgoing pulse. Use the bar below to play, pause or scrub through the rupture.

    The force: 45 delayed copies of the same Ricker

    bz(x, z, t) = F ∑k ŵk r(t − τk) gk(x, z)

    bz: vertical body force · F: force amplitude, per metre of fault (the model is 2D) · k: subsource, 1 … 45 · ŵk: its share of the force (the shares add up to 1) · r: the Ricker pulse · τk: when the rupture front reaches it · gk: how its force spreads over the nearby cells. By linearity, the wavefield is the sum of the 45 wavefields.

    Share of the force along the fault, ŵk

    Elliptical, as the slip of a crack under a uniform stress drop (Eshelby): largest in the middle, zero at the tips. A bar lights when the front reaches it.

    When the front reaches each point, τk

    The rupture speed follows the local Vs and slows down-dip. The horizontal line is the time on the bar.

    The pulse, r(t)

    A Ricker wavelet, f0 = 5 Hz, delayed t0 = 0.3 s so it starts from rest. F is fixed once for the whole family (median PGA 0.4 g at calibration): recalibrating it per sample would divide out exactly the site amplification the network must learn.

    One finite-difference simulation: the velocity–stress equations of elastic P-SV waves on a staggered grid, a free surface on the terrain and absorbing layers (CPML) on the other three sides. This is the real wavefield of the medium, replayed from the database by the repository's own solver and checked against the stored records. The dataset keeps only what the 39 receivers record; the network learns that, never this field.
